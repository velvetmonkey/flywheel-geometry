# Body corporate disputes — strata notes

Apartment block has a body corporate / strata committee. Meets quarterly. Most of the work is benign but a recurring set of disputes keeps surfacing and they're worth thinking about as a pattern, not as individual problems.

**The current disputes**:
1. **Recurring water ingress in unit 7**. Three years running. Each time, the committee debates whether it's structural (common-property responsibility) or in-unit (owner's problem). Each time the answer is unclear, an inspection costs $1,200, and the conclusion is mealy. Real fix would require investigating the building envelope, which nobody wants to fund.
2. **Pool closure for "weather damage"** that's actually accumulated maintenance debt. The committee has been deferring real maintenance for 5 years; calling it weather damage gets the special-levy approved more easily than calling it neglect. Honesty would cost less but creates worse politics.
3. **Visitor parking abuse**. The bylaws are clear; enforcement is weak. The same three units violate consistently; the committee won't issue formal notices because two of the three are committee members.

**The structural problem**: the body corporate operates on consensus among self-interested parties with limited time. Decisions that would be obvious for a single rational owner — "fix the building envelope, raise levies, accept the cost" — never happen because the political cost to whichever committee member proposes it is unbounded. The system isn't broken; it's working as designed, and the design is weak.

**What I keep almost forgetting**: this is not unique to strata. Any decision-making body operating by consensus among self-interested parties with limited time has the same failure mode. Investment committees, academic department meetings, large families dividing inheritance. The pattern produces predictable underinvestment in maintenance and predictable preference for blame-shifting language ("weather damage") over honesty.

**The actionable takeaway**: in any such system, the leverage point is to *reduce the political cost of honest framing*. If "the pool needs a $40k overhaul because we deferred maintenance for 5 years" is politically punishing to say, the system stays stuck. If you can rework norms so that saying it is *expected*, the structure unblocks. Hard. Slow. The kind of work that takes years and is invisible until suddenly things start getting fixed.

**Connection to investing**: this is exactly the dynamic that makes corporate governance reform so slow. Boards, like strata committees, are consensus bodies of self-interested actors. The people best positioned to call out problems have the most to lose by doing so. The reform path is always cultural before procedural — change what's politically sayable, then the procedures follow.
