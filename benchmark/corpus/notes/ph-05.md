# Notes on Stoic exposure therapy

Reading Hadot's *Philosophy as a Way of Life* alongside Marcus Aurelius for the third time, with one particular thread in mind: the Stoic *praemeditatio malorum* — the deliberate rehearsal of misfortunes.

The standard read of this is that it's about emotional preparation: imagine the loss, so the actual loss is less devastating. Marcus on his son's death, etc. There's something to that.

But re-reading with a quant's brain on, I think the practice does something more specific: *it generates synthetic data points outside your normal experiential distribution.* Most lived experience is in-distribution. The stoic exercise deliberately samples from the tails. The point isn't only emotional — it's epistemic. You're improving your model of your own reactions by sampling regions of state-space your daily life doesn't visit.

This is structurally identical to backtesting strategies on *out-of-distribution* historical data. A strategy backtested only on the 2010s is a strategy with no information about 2008-style stress. Forcing the backtest to include 2008 (and 1998, and 1973) is the praemeditatio of strategy design — synthetic-but-grounded sampling of stress regimes that the live trader hasn't seen.

The Stoics weren't doing it for risk-management reasons (they had different motivations), but the *function* of the practice is the same: extending the operating envelope of your own decision-making by rehearsing conditions that aren't currently present.

**Practical**: my own praemeditatio practice is mostly accidental and would be more useful if I made it deliberate. Currently I do it spontaneously when reading about other people's disasters — what would I have done in their shoes? It would be more rigorous to schedule it: once a quarter, an hour spent imagining specific concrete stress scenarios (Meridian losing a major LP, Caspian becoming permanently lame, my partner Robin becoming chronically ill, my own loss of cognitive function), and walking through the response in detail.

The goal isn't to depress oneself. It's to *de-rehearse the shock*. When something approximating a rehearsed scenario actually happens, the freeze-and-panic phase is shorter because some of the cognitive work is already done.

**Connection to riding**: Priya makes us do this with horse-related emergencies. *"What do you do if Caspian rears under saddle?"* — walked through twice a year, kept fresh. I've never had to use it. Means nothing about whether the practice was wasted; means the rehearsal worked.
