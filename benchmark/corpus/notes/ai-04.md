# Linear vs nonlinear concept geometry — survey notes

Reading list and observations.

**Linear representation hypothesis (Park, Jiang, et al.)**: concepts in language models are encoded as directions in activation space. Steering by adding a direction shifts the output along that concept axis. Empirically supported for many concepts — sentiment, gender, nationality.

**Where it breaks**: as soon as the concept has internal structure (cyclic, hierarchical, ordinal-but-not-linear), the linear story stops capturing what the model actually does. Geodesic interpolation experiments show models stay coherent when steering follows the manifold's curve, and break when steering cuts through the void between curve segments.

**Goodfire's geometry zoo**: circles for periodic concepts, helices for years, Fourier features in specific neurons performing actual sinusoidal transforms, shells for social roles, quadrants for moral concepts, phylogenetic trees for biological taxa. The geometry isn't speculative — it's recoverable with the right tools.

**The duality (Hindupur–Lubana–Fel–Ba 2025)**: SAEs impose structural priors that determine what concepts can be detected. Different SAE architectures see different things. There's no neutral interpretability tool — every method is a question, and every question shapes the answer.

**Open question for me**: does this matter for retrieval? Or is it strictly a control / steering concern? My intuition is it does matter, because retrieval is implicitly a "find things on the same manifold neighbourhood" operation, and the neighbourhood structure is what curvature changes. But I have to prove it on a benchmark or it stays intuition.

The pattern across all of this is a shift from *"concepts are points"* to *"concepts are positions on a structured surface."* That second framing is more expensive to work with — non-Euclidean, often non-stationary — but appears to track what models actually do. The cost is justified if the downstream tasks reward respecting the structure.
