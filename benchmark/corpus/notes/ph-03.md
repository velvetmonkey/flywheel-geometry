# Why I distrust closed taxonomies

A perennial source of trouble in finance: the idea that the world divides cleanly into pre-named categories. Asset classes. Investment strategies. Risk factors. The taxonomies pretend to be the structure of the territory, but they're the structure of last decade's *understanding* of the territory.

Closed taxonomies have a specific failure mode: they push thinking into the categories you have rather than the ones you'd need. Once your reporting line says "this strategy is a momentum strategy," you start defending its momentum-ness rather than asking what it actually does. The label colonises the work.

Open taxonomies — fuzzy categories, hybrid descriptors, multi-axis labels — are uglier and harder to work with. They don't fit cleanly in databases. They confuse new joiners. They're also closer to true. The strategies that produce real returns over decades tend to be hybrids that don't fit neatly anywhere — and the labels we eventually invent for them ("trend-following with carry," "convexity-tilted relative-value") are awkward exactly because they cross category lines.

**Connection to interpretability**: the SAE-vs-geometry argument is the same shape. SAEs assume the underlying concept space is *cleanly partitioned* into discrete features. The empirical evidence is that real concept space is full of nonlinear coupled structures — circles, helices, shells. Imposing a partition tells you what the partition allows you to see, and hides everything else.

**Connection to riding**: every dressage test is a closed taxonomy of "movements." Real horsemanship lives in the transitions between them. The judging system has to award marks for movements because that's measurable; the actual quality of riding lives in the un-judgeable spaces. Caspian doesn't think of his work as a sequence of movements. He thinks (or whatever the equivalent is) in terms of balance, rhythm, attention. The taxonomy isn't his.

**The discipline this implies**: when picking up any new field, ask "what are the official categories" — *and then* ask "what work falls between or across them." That's where the unclaimed value usually is. Often the categories were drawn at a moment when the field was in a particular state, and the world has moved on but the categories haven't.

The cost of this discipline is that you sound vague to people whose career is built on the official taxonomies. The reward is occasionally seeing things others can't.
