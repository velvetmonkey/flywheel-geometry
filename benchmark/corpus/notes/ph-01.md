# On the texture of attention

A thing I've been turning over: attention isn't a single quantity, it's a *texture*. There's the wide diffuse attention of scanning a market open — many things at low resolution. There's the narrow point attention of debugging a specific factor model bug. There's the rhythmic alternating attention of riding — the cycle of contact, release, contact, release. There's the back-of-mind attention that keeps a problem alive for days while you do other things.

These are different physiological states, and they're not interchangeable. Trying to debug a model with the diffuse attention you use for market opens is hopeless. Trying to scan markets with debugging-attention misses everything peripheral.

The thing nobody tells you in training as a quant is that *picking the right attention texture is a meta-skill*. The visible job is the analytic work; the invisible job is constantly re-tuning the kind of attention you're bringing.

What I've noticed is that the texture-tuning is often somatic before it's cognitive. Tense shoulders → you've been doing point-attention too long. Slack jaw → you're back in diffuse mode whether you wanted to be or not. The body is the interface to the attention texture, not the cognition. You shape attention by changing posture, breath, muscular tension.

Riding made this obvious in a way nothing else has. Caspian responds to my attention texture before he responds to any cue I'm consciously giving. If I'm carrying tight point-attention from a stressful market session, he reads "controlled, narrow, unsafe" and reacts. If I shift into rhythmic alternating attention before mounting, he reads "centred, communicative" and we have a chance.

**The deeper claim**: every kind of skilled performance has its own attention-texture signature, and improving in any field is partly the discovery of which texture serves the work. The texture isn't separable from the technique. Coaches who teach posture and breath instead of "concentrate harder" are quietly teaching texture-shaping.

This isn't woo. It's the operational physiology of attention. Eventually I'd like to see this written down in a way that doesn't trigger the *eye-roll reflex* in serious-thinking people. Possibly there's a quantitative version waiting somewhere in the EEG / heart-rate-variability literature, but the experiential reality is solid regardless.
