# Exploration vs exploitation in groundwork

The horse-training literature is mostly *exploitation* literature — exercises proven to produce specific outcomes, drilled until reliable. There's a quieter strand of training thinking that's about *exploration* — letting the horse offer behaviours you didn't ask for, then noticing which ones to reinforce.

The tension is real. Pure exploitation produces a reliable but brittle horse — works the cued exercises, panics outside the trained envelope. Pure exploration produces a curious horse with no reliable cues. Neither extreme is what I want.

What seems to work is **scheduled exploration**. 80% of every session is structured: warmup, planned exercises, cool-down. 20% is open-ended — I cue an idea and wait without insisting, see what Caspian offers. Sometimes nothing. Sometimes he offers something I didn't think of: a transition I hadn't planned, a willingness to bend in a direction I hadn't asked for. Those moments are gold and I write them down.

The mathematical analogy is the explore/exploit tradeoff in bandit problems and in RL. Greedy policies converge fast but get trapped in local optima. ε-greedy explores enough to find better policies. The 80/20 in groundwork is essentially ε ≈ 0.2, except the "rewards" being explored aren't just behaviours — they're the *shape of the relationship between handler and horse*. You're searching policy space and rapport space simultaneously.

This connects to something Marcus said about research careers: "Drill the technique you have, but reserve a fraction of your time for problems your technique doesn't solve. The drilled fraction pays the rent. The exploration fraction is where the next ten years come from." Same shape.

What I'd love to find: a principled way to know when to up ε. With Caspian, I think it's when the planned exercises become *too* automatic — the relationship is fine but stops compounding. Same diagnostic with research: when the work is getting easier but not more interesting, push exploration up.
