# What craftsmen know about feedback

Reading a chapter on tool use among Japanese carpenters. The thing that struck me: their training isn't only about technique. It's about the *quality of feedback* the tool gives.

A sharp chisel cuts cleanly and leaves a surface that *tells you* whether your angle was right. A dull chisel tears, but the tear hides whatever your angle did. So the apprentice's first job — for years — is sharpening. Not because dull chisels can't cut, but because *dull chisels lie*. They don't return enough information to learn from.

The same shape applies everywhere. A factor model with unclean input data lies — it generates returns whose attribution is muddled, so you can't tell whether the model worked or whether you got lucky. A horse who's been pushed past the point of attention lies — he'll perform exercises mechanically even when the underlying balance is wrong, so you can't tell whether your aids are clean. A debugger whose output is buried in irrelevant log lines lies — the actual bug's signature is invisible in the noise.

In every case, the *first* improvement isn't about doing the thing better. It's about getting the feedback channel clean enough that doing the thing can compound. Craftsmen know this. Quants relearn it every few years. Trainers (good ones) treat it as the foundation. Programmers tend to underestimate it until they've burned a week on a bug that better logging would have made trivial.

**Generalised version**: the rate at which any skill compounds is bounded by the quality of the feedback channel. Improving the channel produces gains that look exponential because every subsequent practice session converts to learning more efficiently.

**Concrete daily practice**: when starting a new project, spend the first day on instrumentation. When starting work with a new horse, spend the first sessions establishing communication. When taking on a new analyst, spend the first month on shared definitions of what "good" looks like. This always feels like wasted time and is always the highest-leverage time in the project.

**The shadow side**: people who never invest in feedback channels can still produce work, but their growth flattens early. They mistake "I've been doing this 20 years" for expertise. Twenty years of muddied feedback ≠ twenty years of compounding improvement.
