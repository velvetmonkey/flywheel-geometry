# Why SAEs miss helices

Working through the duality argument. Sparse autoencoders are *trained* to find sparse linear factorisations. That training objective imposes a prior: every concept has an independent direction, the directions tile the activation space, and a small number of directions explain any given activation.

The problem is that human concepts are not always shaped that way. Days of the week sit on a circle. Months sit on a circle. The concept "September" is not orthogonal to "August" or "October" — it lives geometrically *between* them. An SAE trained on residual stream activations will find directions that *correlate* with each month, but the directions are shards of the underlying ring rather than the ring itself. Steering along any one direction takes you off the ring into the void.

This generalises. Any concept with a periodic, helical, or cyclically-coupled structure is going to get shattered by SAEs. Years of a century → helix. Hours of a day → circle. Emotional valence-arousal space → roughly elliptical surface. Social roles → shells in some power-warmth axis pair. The interpretability tool you pick determines what you can see. If your only tool is sparse linear directions, you only see things that decompose linearly.

The implication for retrieval is direct: if I'm trying to find conceptual neighbours of a note that's "about Septembers" in some sense, an SAE-derived neighbourhood metric will treat any month as roughly equally close. A geometrically faithful method should know September is *between* August and October on the manifold. Whether anyone's built that retrieval method yet is the open question.

Compare to factor models in finance: the principal-components-style decomposition assumes orthogonal factors. But economic regimes interact — credit and rates aren't independent, they're coupled through risk appetite. Linear factor decomposition misses the coupling. Same family of mistakes.
